NHSE role: orchestrator, not party

NHSE convenes, assures and introduces. The contract sits between supplier and customer. NHSE takes no percentage of the bilateral transaction.

StatusActive
OwnerHomeTest Commercial lead
Date taken28 May 2026
Review cadenceQuarterly portfolio review

Position

NHSE's role in the interim commercial machinery is to convene the system, assure supplier quality against the published standard, and introduce approved suppliers to commissioners. NHSE is not a party to the resulting contract between supplier and commissioner. NHSE does not take a percentage or fee from the bilateral transaction. The transaction sits between supplier and customer.

Rationale

At interim portfolio scale of one to three live tests and sub-£10m annual transacted volume, any percent NHSE took from the bilateral would be small in absolute terms and politically heavy in proportion. The implied trade (NHSE absorbs commercial liability, takes a slice, runs the framework administration) costs more in standup and ongoing overhead than the percent earns.

Standing outside the bilateral reduces NHSE's commercial liability for what commissioners and suppliers do, while preserving NHSE's authority over the approved list and the assurance standard. If a supplier underperforms or a commissioner exits, NHSE is the convenor and the assurance authority, not the commercial counterparty being sued.

The matchmaker-without-percent stance also lets NHSE focus its scarce resource on what it uniquely can do: convene the system, hold the assurance standard, run the joint forum with IG/DPO, host the approved list. Commercial mechanics that any commissioner could run with the standard call-off do not need NHSE to be in the middle of them.

Reopen triggers

  • Portfolio scale reaches the framework-reopen threshold (around ten live tests, £10m+ volume per Paper 1) and a take-rate becomes worthwhile.
  • A regulatory change requires NHSE to be a party to the contract for a specific scenario.
  • A patient-harm or supplier-failure event implicates NHSE as a party regardless of the formal model. At that point the formal model needs to follow the practical reality.
  • Strategic Finance or Treasury direction requires NHSE to operate as a revenue-collecting counterparty.

Dependencies

  • Approved-list governance settled. Admission, removal, appeal, audit. The non-party stance requires the list itself to carry credibility.
  • Standard call-off template makes the supplier-commissioner structure explicit. The contract names NHSE as the issuer of the template and the holder of the assurance standard, not as a contracting party.
  • Communication discipline. Commissioners and suppliers need to understand the structure. A clear statement on the front door and inside the standard call-off prevents drift to "NHSE is in the middle of this".

What this stance does not mean

NHSE is not absent. It curates the approved list, sets and revises the assurance standard, runs the joint IG forum, leads the engagement front end (see Position · Pre-sales and engagement cell), holds the brand and the political accountability for the programme. Standing outside the bilateral contract is a structural choice about commercial liability and the take-rate, not a withdrawal from convening authority. Under the enduring model this is Option B, Platform and Market Infrastructure: NHSE owns the platform, sets and holds the assurance standards, and is the contracting authority for the framework, while staying outside the payment flow. The framework is funded by a supplier-paid levy of 1 to 2 per cent of supplier revenue through it, trending to 1 per cent, not a cut of the bilateral. Commissioners hold the budget and call off locally.
