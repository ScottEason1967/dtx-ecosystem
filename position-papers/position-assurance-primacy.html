Supplier admission: assured once, then each test at its gate

A supplier is approved once at company level and then per test category as gates open on a published roadmap. Assurance is what makes the framework worth joining; the commercial machinery is built on top of it.

StatusAgreed in principle · roadmap not yet agreed
OwnerHomeTest Commercial lead with the Independent Assurance lead
Date taken13 August 2026 (two-tier model); 28 May 2026 (assurance primacy)
Review cadenceAt each reopening window and on the roadmap decision

Position

Suppliers join the framework through two tiers of assurance, and the framework grows by opening capability gates on a published roadmap, never by adding lots. Tier 1 admits the company once: corporate structure, technical and operational readiness including the one-off platform connection, cyber, information governance and clinical safety governance, mostly through the Master SCAL, plus financial standing added by the framework. Tier 2 approves the company for a test category when that category’s gate opens: clinical evidence including non-inferiority, UKAS accreditation for the named test, MHRA-conformant kit, and API and journey conformance. Both approvals are reusable with every commissioner who calls off; the local body deploys and does not re-assure. Gates open at reopening windows, annual as the working assumption. A category opens only when demand, platform readiness and enough of the supply market line up; a category one laboratory could serve stays shut. About three suppliers per test is the working floor. Suppliers come in as a prime, a consortium or an NHS-led arrangement, and partnering is welcome.

Rationale

Assurance is primary and commercial is secondary. Commissioners and patients do not buy introductions; they buy NHS England’s underwriting that a supplier meets a published standard. Remove the assurance and the framework is a directory anyone could run. The commercial layer is what gives the standard teeth and lets it scale, so both are necessary, but investment and attention sequence assurance first.

Manual onboarding will not scale. The process that admitted the first suppliers was hands-on: staged gates, a dozen reviewing functions, manual witness testing. That fails for an open framework that reopens at windows, and holding a supplier in a queue on a public framework is a legal risk, not a control. Entry has to be published, largely automated and quick, with a clinician panel judging the clinical bar and automated conformance taking the technical one.

Opening gates rather than adding lots is the move that ties the legal constraint, the lotting decision and the roadmap into one idea. Under an open framework you cannot add or redesign lots on reopening, only add or drop suppliers; new capability therefore arrives by opening a gate in the single lot. How many accredited suppliers each category holds sets the competitive tension, and so the realised price against the national ceiling.

What this does not mean

Tier 1 does not expire with a gate. A supplier that misses a gate applies at the next one; company approval stands.

The register is not the roadmap. The roadmap says which categories will open and when; the register says who can sell what now. The roadmap is not yet agreed, and nothing in the model invents its contents.

Any Qualified Provider with a soft floor of about three per test and no hard cap is the current steer. A cap to enable volume aggregation sits in live tension with it; both are modelled and neither is presented as decided.

Reopen triggers

  • The first published roadmap is agreed, or the reopening cadence is settled (annual advised by providers; six months requested by the programme, reasons being played back).
  • Accreditation automation proves slower or costlier than assumed, or the clinician panel cannot be staffed with practising clinicians.
  • A category cannot reach the supplier floor, or one supplier dominates a category.
  • MHRA, UKAS or DTAC requirements change the Tier 2 evidence set.

Dependencies and open items

  • The published roadmap of test categories and gate dates, far enough ahead for a laboratory to book and win accreditation.
  • Automated conformance testing and the clinician panel, with its cost and cadence.
  • Even-handedness between NHS and independent suppliers, and the community of practice for organisations that build rather than buy.
  • Independent assurance stood up as a discipline separate from internal clinical safety; DCB0129 and DCB0160 split clean; MHRA confirmed the platform is not a medical device (June 2026), so platform-layer assurance sits outside device regulation while kits and laboratories stay inside it.
