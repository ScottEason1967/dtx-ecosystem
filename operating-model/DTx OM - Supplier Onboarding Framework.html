The supplier-side mechanic that makes the model scale

Supplier Onboarding Framework.

Status. Rebuilt to the sourcing strategy position agreed in principle: a preferred single end-to-end lot (being stress-tested against a multi-lot approach), a two-layer assurance model, and capability gates opening on a published roadmap. Decided in principle, not settled design. The open items are marked at the foot of the page, and the detailed assurance stages still need iteration with the programme team across IG, Clinical Safety, Commercial and Platform.
Portfolio note. The mechanics on this page are the home self-sampling framework's, so HomeTest. HealthStore admission runs on its own conditional route and does not use these gates; HealthSync remains reserved. What reads across the portfolio is the principle: assure at the level the evidence attaches to, once.

A supplier passes company-level assurance once. That is the ticket onto the framework. Selling any given test takes a second pass: the per-category test and capability assurance, available only when that category's gate opens on the published roadmap. Both certificates are portable across every commissioner who calls off. The local body deploys; it does not re-assure.

The north star this delivers

Assure once. Reuse nationally.

Assurance happens at the level the evidence attaches to, and it happens once. Company assurance is once per supplier: the ticket onto the framework. Test and capability assurance is once per category: the key that lets a supplier sell it. Deployment context stays local because the law puts it there, and it arrives pre-fabricated from the centre. Nobody repeats anybody's work, and no commissioner re-assures what the framework has already assured.

Why this exists

The process that admitted the first suppliers cannot be the process at scale.

The onboarding that admitted the first suppliers is real and rigorous: staged gates, a dozen reviewing functions, manual witness testing, hands-on integration support. It works for a handful of suppliers. It does not work for an open framework with gates opening roughly every six months, and there is a harder edge here than convenience. A supplier held in an assurance queue is being kept off a public framework. It can challenge, and a sustained failure could close the framework down. So the process has to be slick, semi-automated and published, with human judgement reserved for the calls that need it. Capacity problems get answered with automation and published lead times, never by quietly rationing entry.

The two-layer assurance model

One ticket. Many keys.

Everything a supplier evidences sits in one of two layers. The first is about the company and happens once. The second is about each test category and happens per category, at a gate. Keeping them apart is what stops the framework re-assuring companies every time a test comes on, and what stops a company-level pass ever being mistaken for clinical readiness on a given test.

Layer 1 · The ticket
Supplier assurance. Once per supplier.

Company standing and hygiene: legal entity, solvency, insurance, ownership and conflicts declared. Cyber security and information governance: security management, toolkit compliance, a working penetration testing regime, DPIA readiness. Business continuity and viability, with exit arrangements. Clinical safety governance at company level: a clinical safety officer in post and a risk management process that works.

Passed once at framework entry, confirmed on a light annual cycle, revisited only when something material changes.

What it gets you: admission to the framework and a place on the register. It sells nothing on its own.
Layer 2 · The key
Test and capability assurance. Per category.

Clinical evidence that capillary self-sampling is non-inferior to the venous draw for the analyte, judged by a clinician panel convened for the gate. Laboratory accreditation that names the test, the method and the site. Kit conformity for the assembled device, regulated and registered as the diagnostic it is. Platform and API integration proven against published, versioned specifications.

Granted per category, while that category's gate is open. A supplier holds as many keys as it can evidence.

What it gets you: the right to sell that category through the framework. The register shows who holds which keys.
The gates

Categories switch on at gates. Suppliers walk through them.

The published roadmap decides which test categories exist and when each gate opens, roughly every six months. A category that is not on the roadmap cannot be accredited for, however ready the supplier. When a gate opens, any supplier holding the ticket can seek the key for one category or several. A supplier that is not ready walks through a later gate; the ticket does not expire with the gate. Three things decide whether a category opens: demand strategy, the platform's own readiness to integrate the test and its pathway, and whether enough of the supply market can serve it. A category only one laboratory can serve does not open. It waits until competition can exist.

Step 1
Roadmap publishes
Categories and gate dates land far enough ahead for a laboratory to book and win the accreditation it needs.
Step 2
Gate opens
The category joins the framework's catalogue. Ticket holders can now seek its key.
Step 3
Suppliers accredit
Per-category evidence goes in. The clinician panel judges the clinical bar; automated conformance takes the technical one.
Step 4
Register updates
The accredited register shows who can sell what. About three suppliers per test is the working floor.
Step 5
Commissioners call off
Local call-offs run against the register, at or below the national ceiling. More keys in a category means more price tension.
The roadmap is also the volume engine and the pricing engine. Which categories are open decides what volume can flow, and how many suppliers hold each key decides how hard price gets pushed beneath the national ceiling. The mechanism lives on the Prioritisation Framework page.

Ways onto the framework

One lot. Three ways to stand behind it.

The framework buys the whole journey, request to result at home, as a preferred single end-to-end lot, agreed in principle and being stress-tested against a multi-lot approach, with a working name of home self-sampling as a service. Nobody has to own every capability. Somebody has to be accountable for all of it.

The prime
Offers the full journey. Answers for all of it.
Carries responsibility for every part of the journey, including the parts it subcontracts. The market already sells the journey end to end, so the framework buys it end to end, with a minimum set of components in the box.
The consortium
Specialists behind a nominated prime.
The route in for the laboratory, the logistics specialist and the niche innovator that cannot span the journey alone. Partnering is invited, not tolerated. The single-accountability line only holds if one member is the nominated prime.
The NHS-led bid
Not precluded.
An NHS organisation, pathology networks included, can bid alone or in partnership on the same terms as anyone else, arranging its own joined-up service to do it.
Any qualified provider. No hard cap on numbers and no ambition to ration entry. The working floor is about three accredited suppliers per test before a category is worth opening, and the natural throttle is how few laboratories currently hold accreditation that names capillary collection. Fitness is assessed; throughput is never rationed.
Insourcers sit off-framework, on purpose. An NHS organisation that stands up its own home sampling instead of buying it is a success for the programme, not a competitor to it. The support for those organisations is deliberately not a lot: a community of practice and concierge, NHS-controlled, helping a provider stand itself up and, in time, sell to its neighbours. The framework stays about buying; the concierge is about becoming.
The journey, gate by gate

Published first. Automated where it can be. Human where it must be.

A supplier can read everything, self-assess and decide whether to proceed before anyone engages. The amber-bordered stages are where the framework's assurance actually runs.

1
Discovery
The criteria for both layers and the category roadmap are published. A supplier reads the bar and the timetable before spending anything.
2
Self-assessment
Structured self-assessment against the published pack, using the template artefacts. No engagement needed to reach a view.
3
Apply at a gate
An application lands against an open gate, declaring the categories sought and the evidence held.
4
The ticket
Company-level assurance runs once: automated triage on completeness, human review where judgement is needed. Time-boxed, with a published turnaround.Framework entry
5
The keys
Per-category evidence is judged. The clinician panel takes the clinical bar; automated conformance takes the technical one.Category gate
6
Integrate and witness
Platform integration against the versioned specifications, then a witnessed clinical journey where a human genuinely needs to watch. Go-live sign-off ends it.Go live
7
Later gates
Adding a category is the key process only, at any open gate. The ticket is confirmed annually and never repeated. Exit runs to a defined offboarding process.
Seven design principles

What the framework holds itself to.

Each principle is load-bearing. The whole framework weakens if any one of them is dropped in the build.

Principle 01
Two layers, not one bar.
Company assurance once per supplier. Test and capability assurance once per category. Keeping them separate is what stops re-assurance creeping in at either level.
Principle 02
Publish the criteria. Do not gate them.
Criteria and roadmap live in the open. A supplier reads, self-assesses and decides before any engagement. Publication exposes the design to challenge, which improves it.
Principle 03
Automate the checkable. Reserve the judgement.
Completeness, conformance and technical proof run without a human. People take the grey areas: clinical safety, information governance, the borderline calls. Where that line falls sets the cost and the queue.
Principle 04
The roadmap is the fairness mechanism.
Gates and categories publish far enough ahead that any capable laboratory can get ready. A gate that opens without lead time is a queue with a nicer name.
Principle 05
Assess fitness. Never ration throughput.
Any qualified provider means the answer to capacity pressure is automation and better process, not barriers. A queue is a legal risk, not a control.
Principle 06
Every artefact exists once.
DPIA template, data sharing template, privacy wording, safety case starter, integration guide. One version of each, held centrally, inherited everywhere.
Principle 07
Supplier experience is a design input.
The journey is designed from the supplier's side first. If a capable supplier cannot find the bar, cannot self-assess or cannot apply without a private conversation, the design has failed.
Where this stands

Answered in principle. Open in detail.

The shape below is agreed in principle from the sourcing strategy work and is firm enough to build to. None of it is settled enough to tender on, and the detail underneath it is live work.

Open 01
The stages in detail, and how much automates.
The straw-man assurance process is being recirculated with a view on exactly what can run without a human. The dividing line between automated conformance and human judgement sets the cost, the queue and what the levy has to fund.
Open 02
One door or two.
Whether framework entry and category accreditation present to a supplier as one journey or two, and how the centre's assurance and the framework provider's process mesh without duplicating either.
Open 03
The test standard itself.
What the per-category bar actually cites: accreditation scope, the recognised standards, and a route for capable laboratories that do not yet hold them. Being defined with the clinical and laboratory community.
Open 04
Cadence and lead time.
Roughly six-monthly is the working rhythm. The exact reopening cadence, and how far ahead the roadmap must publish so accreditation booking times do not swallow the window, are still to be set.
Open 05
Live before fully plugged in.
Whether a supplier can start delivering ahead of full platform integration, and what that would do to the eligibility levers that make framework membership worth having.
