Supplier onboarding for HomeTest

Supplier Onboarding Framework.

Status. Rebuilt to the sourcing strategy position agreed in principle: a preferred single end-to-end lot (being stress-tested against a multi-lot approach), a two-tier assurance model, and capability gates opening on a published roadmap. Decided in principle, not settled design. The open items and the working detail live on the detail page linked at the foot.
Scope. This page is HomeTest only: onboarding for the home self-sampling framework.

A supplier is approved once at company level: Tier 1, framework entry. Selling a given test needs a second approval for that test category: Tier 2, available when the category's gate opens on the published roadmap. Both approvals are reusable with every commissioner who calls off; the local body deploys and does not re-assure. The step-by-step working detail sits on its own page.

Why this exists

Manual onboarding will not scale.

The process that admitted the first suppliers was hands-on: staged gates, a dozen reviewing functions, manual witness testing. That works for a handful of suppliers and fails for an open framework that reopens roughly every six months. Entry has to be published, largely automated and quick, because holding a supplier in a queue on a public framework is a legal risk, not a control.

The two-tier assurance model

Companies are approved once; each test category is approved separately.

Everything a supplier has to show sits in one of two tiers, and most of it is carried on a single NHS instrument, the Master SCAL. Tier 1 covers the company and is passed once, on the SCAL's company tab. Tier 2 covers each test category and is passed at its gate, on the SCAL's service tab. The framework then adds what the SCAL does not reach, the commercial judgement at Tier 1 and the diagnostic evidence at Tier 2. Keeping the tiers separate means companies are never re-assured when a new test comes on, and a company-level pass is never mistaken for clinical readiness on a given test.

On the Master SCAL Added by the framework
Tier 1 · Framework entry

Company-level assurance, passed once.

At the first gate, a supplier shows who it is, that it is sound, and that it can operate safely, under six headings.

Corporate structure

Legal entity, ownership, and conflicts declared.

Financial standing

Solvency and insurance. Business continuity and viability, with exit arrangements.

Technical and operational capability

Service management, incident routes, service desk registration.

Cyber security

Security management and a working penetration testing regime.

Information governance

Toolkit compliance, lawful basis, DPIA readiness.

Clinical safety governance

A clinical safety officer in post, a compliant risk management process, incident reporting and regulator notification routes.

Governance sits here. Clinical evidence for any test sits in Tier 2.
Passed once at framework entry, confirmed on a light annual cycle, revisited only when something material changes. What it gives: admission to the framework and a place on the register. It does not by itself allow any test to be sold.
Tier 2 · Test approval

Test and capability assurance, per category at its gate.

Each test category carries its own assurance criteria, available only when that category's gate opens on the published roadmap. Different tests bring different bars.

Clinical evidence

Capillary self-sampling shown to be non-inferior to the venous draw for the analyte, judged by a clinician panel convened for the gate.

Laboratory accreditation

Accreditation that names the test, the method and the site.

Kit conformity

The assembled device regulated and registered as the diagnostic it is.

Platform integration

API and journey integration proven against published, versioned specifications.

Granted per category, while that category's gate is open. A supplier can hold approvals for as many categories as it can evidence. What it gives: the right to sell that category through the framework. The register shows which suppliers are approved for which categories.
The gates

Test categories open at gates.

Which test categories exist, and when each gate opens (roughly every six months), is decided by the published roadmap. However ready a supplier is, a category off the roadmap cannot be accredited for. When a gate opens, any supplier with Tier 1 approval can apply for one category or several, and a supplier that is not ready applies at a later gate instead; Tier 1 approval does not expire with the gate. Whether a category opens turns on three things: demand strategy, the platform's own readiness to integrate the test and its pathway, and whether enough of the supply market can serve it. A category only one laboratory can serve does not open. It stays closed until competition can exist.

Step 1
Roadmap publishes
Categories and gate dates land far enough ahead for a laboratory to book and win the accreditation it needs.
Step 2
Gate opens
The category joins the framework's catalogue. Suppliers with Tier 1 approval can now apply.
Step 3
Suppliers accredit
Per-category evidence goes in. The clinician panel judges the clinical bar; automated conformance takes the technical one.
Step 4
Register updates
The accredited register shows who can sell what. About three suppliers per test is the working floor.
Step 5
Commissioners call off
Local call-offs run against the register, at or below the national ceiling. More approved suppliers in a category means more price tension.
The roadmap also sets volume and price. What volume can flow depends on which categories are open, and how hard price gets pushed beneath the national ceiling depends on how many suppliers are approved for each category. The mechanism lives on the Prioritisation Framework page.
The roadmap

The roadmap is not yet agreed.

The published roadmap lists the test categories and their gate dates. It is the single document a supplier reads to decide whether to invest, what to accredit for, and who to partner with; a consortium cannot form around a pipeline it cannot see. The Prioritisation Framework sets out how categories are chosen and sequenced.

Where it stands open · action

The mechanism exists; the roadmap does not yet. Categories and gate dates have not been agreed. Until they are, suppliers cannot plan accreditation, consortium formation cannot start in earnest, and the volume and pricing engines described above have nothing to run on.

Agreeing the first published roadmap is an open action with the programme. Nothing on this page invents its contents.

Ways onto the framework

One lot, three ways to deliver it.

What the framework buys is the whole journey, request to result at home, as one end-to-end lot, agreed in principle and carrying the working name of home self-sampling as a service. Nobody has to own every capability. Somebody has to be accountable for all of it.

The prime
Offers the full journey and answers for all of it.
Carries responsibility for every part of the journey, including the parts it subcontracts. The market already sells the journey end to end, so the framework buys it end to end, with a minimum set of components in the box.
The consortium
Specialists behind a nominated prime.
The route in for the laboratory, the logistics specialist and the niche innovator that cannot span the journey alone. Partnering is actively encouraged. The single-accountability line only holds if one member is the nominated prime.
The NHS-led bid
Allowed on the same terms.
An NHS organisation, pathology networks included, can bid alone or in partnership on the same terms as anyone else, arranging its own joined-up service to do it.
Any qualified provider. No hard cap on numbers and no ambition to ration entry. The working floor is about three accredited suppliers per test before a category is worth opening, and the practical constraint is how few laboratories currently hold accreditation that names capillary collection. Fitness is assessed, and entry is never rationed.
Insourcers are supported outside the framework. An NHS organisation that stands up its own home sampling instead of buying it is a success for the programme rather than a competitor to it. The support is deliberately not a lot: an NHS-controlled community of practice and concierge that helps a provider stand itself up and, in time, sell to its neighbours. The framework is for buying; the concierge helps organisations become providers.
If capability lots are chosen

Onboarding under the alternative.

The lotting choice is open. If capability lots are chosen instead of one end-to-end lot, Tier 1 does not change: a company is approved once, whatever it does. Tier 2 changes shape completely. Each capability would need its own approval pack, because the evidence that matters is different for each, and a logistics provider and a pathology laboratory would face very different requirements. Specialists would apply straight into their own lot with no prime needed, and pathology networks would get a direct route in.

Logistics provider

Getting kits out and samples back: postal and courier performance, kit integrity in transit, tracking and turnaround evidence. No clinical bar of its own.

Kit manufacturer

The device itself: conformity and registration as the diagnostic it is, the kit quality standard, manufacture and packaging controls.

Pathology laboratory

The analysis: accreditation naming the test, the method and the site, sample handling, and the clinical bar for each analyte.

Digital service

The journey: API and journey conformance against versioned specifications, the pathway's data flows, service management.

Not yet designed open

None of the four packs exists; the sketches above only show where the differences would fall. The four lots cover the delivery chain, kit out, sample back, analysis and the digital journey, but the service wrap around the patient (support when a kit goes missing, the hand-off into the pathway) has no lot of its own: under one lot the provider owns it, under capability lots it would fall to whoever assembles the chain. Two questions therefore sit over all of them: who assembles the parts into a service a commissioner can buy, and who answers for the whole when one part fails. Both would need answers before the alternative could be chosen. The full comparison is on the Lotting Choice page.

Not yet tested with the supplier market

The questions the suppliers get asked.

The mechanics on this page are the programme's design. The supplier market has not yet been asked whether it works for them. These questions go to the next engagement round, and what comes back changes this page.

The questions

  • Would you bid, and in what shape? Alone, as a prime with subcontractors, in a consortium, or into a capability lot if that structure were chosen?
  • What would keep you out? The requirement, the levy, the assurance burden, the timeline, or something we have not seen?
  • Does the levy work? 1 to 2 percent of throughput, on sales only. Does it change your pricing or your appetite?
  • Can you meet the bar, and how long would it take? Laboratory accreditation naming capillary collection is scarce. What lead time do you need to get it?
  • Where should assurance automate? Which parts of the two tiers would human review slow down without adding anything?
  • How far ahead must the roadmap show? How much notice of categories and gate dates do you need to invest, accredit or form a consortium?
Where this stands

Agreed in principle, open in detail.

The shape below is agreed in principle from the sourcing strategy work and is firm enough to build to. None of it is settled enough to tender on, and the detail underneath it is live work.
