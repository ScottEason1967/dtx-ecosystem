The commercial model · the decision that shapes it

The Lotting Choice.

One end-to-end lot, request to result at home, is the position held in principle. The multi-lot alternative, capability lots alongside an end-to-end lot, was assessed and set aside. This page keeps the comparison and the rationale on record, because no call in the sourcing strategy will draw more scrutiny and lots cannot be changed once the framework is live.

Held in principle, with conditions. The position stands unless the supplier and commissioner engagement now under way, or the framework provider decision, produces evidence that changes it.

The two options

Both were assessed. The strategy has to show that, and show that the rationale rests on evidence, so both stay on the page.

Option A · the position held

One end-to-end lot

The whole journey, bought as one package, with a Community of Practice alongside.
  • Commissioners buy an outcome. One accountable provider per call-off.
  • A prime, a consortium or an NHS-led arrangement delivers it.
  • Suppliers holding part of the journey come in by partnering: do what you can, team for the rest.
  • A Community of Practice sits beside the lot, not as a lot. It gives suppliers and NHS bodies who want to build the capability themselves a set of assured standards to build against, plus signposting to kit and box suppliers. It keeps pathology networks and NHS labs in the picture.
  • One assurance construct: the organisation accredited once, then per test at the gates.
The risks. Can be seen as favouring suppliers that already operate end-to-end. Niche suppliers enter by finding partners, so the Community of Practice and active support for forming partnerships have to be part of the model. What the Community of Practice offers, and who runs it, is still open.
Option B · the alternative, assessed and set aside

Capability lots, plus an end-to-end lot

A lot per capability, with the whole service still available as its own lot.
  • Lots drawn by organisational capability: logistics, kits, lab analysis, digital service. The end-to-end results lot is kept alongside them.
  • Commissioners assemble a service from the parts they need, or buy the whole thing through the end-to-end lot.
  • Suppliers enter the lot that fits them, with no need to partner first. Specialists, including NHS labs, come in directly.
  • The integrated proposition is protected: the end-to-end lot means no new hand-offs are forced on anyone who does not want them.
The risks. Stage-two assurance breaks into a pack per capability, each built and run separately. When a service is assembled from parts, someone still has to own the end-to-end result. More providers means more interfaces to manage, and the full multi-lot design is paid for up front whether or not the extra lots fill. Three further concerns were recorded when it was assessed. The framework starts to read as a pathology or logistics framework rather than a home testing service. Rates in lots that are not the framework's core focus would struggle against the established frameworks that already buy pathology and logistics at scale. And the component markets are not shaped for lots, since kits cannot be price-listed and logistics is effectively a single provider.
Lots are fixed on day one. Under the Procurement Act, reopenings add or remove suppliers within the lots that exist, but a new lot cannot be added later. Whatever launches holds for the life of the framework, which is why the choice has to be right first time. It is also why the multi-lot option would define the end-to-end lot and the capability lots together at launch, even if some open with few or no suppliers, so that nothing is shut off.
Why one lot

The rationale on record, in four parts. It rests on how the clinical chain of custody works and on how the market already assembles itself, not on a preference for integrated suppliers.

The clinical chain of custody

From request to result a sample and its data pass through several hands. Somebody has to oversee and curate that chain, and that somebody has to be assured, because there is no acceptable drop-off point. Bought in pieces, no single organisation owns the risk of a sample going missing, a result failing to flow, or a step falling between two contracts. One lot puts that ownership in one place and lets the risk be delegated to the supplier that has taken it on.

The market already works this way

Home testing organisations, independent and increasingly NHS, hold the request-to-result chain as a block and assemble it from specialists. Kits are a medical device and are usually developed and approved through a kit specialist. Logistics is almost universally one tracked carrier. Laboratory analysis is in-house or contracted. Some of the newest entrants own no laboratory and no kit line and have built the chain entirely from contracts. Buying the chain end to end matches how it is sold.

Assurance comes from the pathology system

The claim that a supplier can safely join the chain up is evidenced through UKAS accreditation of the laboratory and MHRA conformity of the kit. The framework requires and checks that; it does not construct it. One lot keeps the requirement uniform across every supplier. Each capability lot would need its own assurance route.

Build or buy, and what a builder still buys

The framework gives NHS organisations a build-or-buy choice and is agnostic between the two. An organisation that builds still tends to buy its kits and its logistics. Neither warrants a lot, because kits vary too much to be price-listed and the logistics market is effectively a single provider, so both are signposted through the community of practice. Component lots run by others, on a wider pathology framework for instance, are welcome alongside; the home testing lot has to keep its identity and its capability to deliver the whole service wherever it is hosted.

Same supplier, two journeys

The straw-man onboarding journey under each option. Neither option removes the friction; it moves. Under Option A it sits before entry, under Option B after entry.

Under one end-to-end lot

1

See the test roadmap and the reopening windows. The timeline is published.

2

Assemble the offer: bid alone, form or join a consortium, or come in NHS-led.

3

Stage one: organisation accreditation, once, onto the framework.

4

Stage two: per-test accreditation as each roadmap gate opens.

5

Supply into call-offs. Paid per activity. Levy applies.

The friction: step 2. Without partners, a capable niche supplier cannot enter. Early roadmap visibility and deliberate introductions address this.

Under capability lots, with an end-to-end lot

1

See the roadmap. Same timeline, same gates.

2

Apply into the capability lot you serve, or into the end-to-end lot. No need to partner first.

3

Stage one accreditation for the lot.

4

Stage two in a capability-specific form: a logistics pack is not a lab pack.

5

Where a service is assembled from parts, it still has to be joined into a chain. Open: who assembles it, and who answers for the whole when a part fails.

6

Supply the component, or the whole service through the end-to-end lot. Paid per activity. Levy applies.

The friction: steps 4 and 5. Every capability lot needs its own assurance pack, and when parts are bought separately the integrator question still has no owner.
Four lenses

Both options were run through each lens. Option A carries three of the four; market shape is the lens where the case has to demonstrate balance.

Assurance and administration

A: one construct, two stages, one pack to maintain.

B: a stage-two pack per capability, more to build and more to administer. This is the strongest internal concern about lots.

Market shape

The market is already modular. Primes assemble kit, logistics, lab and digital from specialist firms, and one kitting and fulfilment specialist handles most UK self-sampling kits today. Under a single lot it could not enter on its own.

The bias risk runs in both directions. A can read as built for end-to-end incumbents. B can read as built for NHS pathology delivery. Either way, the strategy has to demonstrate balance and set out how suppliers not directly served can still participate.

The supply base under one lot is thin for the first two configurations. HIV is effectively two suppliers, with an existing framework already covering the tests. Prostate cancer active surveillance has no end-to-end supplier in the market today. The RFI analysis showed how hard the whole journey is for smaller suppliers to assemble. How many suppliers will actually enter is the question the supplier test has to answer.

What commissioners buy

A: an outcome, with one accountable provider per call-off.

B: either the whole service through the end-to-end lot, or components they assemble themselves, with the integration burden then sitting between buyer and framework.

Commissioner engagement to date, across sexual health, HIV and prostate cancer, has found no buyer wanting to assemble components; those who buy today buy a full pathway. That evidence is being consolidated for the strategy.

Patients and the service

However the market is structured, the patient experiences one journey. A puts accountability for that journey in one place. B has to engineer it. The service has to work under either lotting outcome.

The flexibility challenge, and the answer on record. The market may look different in three to five years, and a single lot cannot be changed. Lots are fixed for the life of the framework, which is why the choice rests on evidence rather than on possibilities. No alternative future has been described in which a request-to-result lot stops being what commissioners need. The expected shift from buying to building is served outside the framework through the community of practice, and new test types that may emerge in the framework's lifetime are not mature enough to lot for now. The position stays open to challenge if evidence appears.
What still has to land

The conditions outstanding

  • The supplier test. The framework principles, including the lot shape, go to suppliers at the next market engagement, and the feedback goes on record for governance. A single lot with only two or three suppliers on it would not survive scrutiny, so supplier commitment is the first condition.
  • The commissioner evidence. Engagement to date reports no demand for component buying. That is being consolidated into evidence the strategy can cite.
  • The framework provider decision. If the lot is hosted inside a wider pathology framework, it has to keep its identity and its capability to deliver the whole service, and the levy and the community of practice have to be designed with that in mind. The joint session with the prospective provider is the next step.
  • The contract construct. The working theory is that the provider carries framework assurance and NHSE mandates baseline standards through a direct relationship with each supplier, along the lines of the GP IT catalogue agreement. Being worked through with legal.
  • Even-handedness. Supporting NHS organisations to build while running an open route for independent suppliers needs internal separation, so the programme cannot be accused of favouring one side or passing learning across.
The test the rationale has to pass. A single lot chosen because it suits incumbent end-to-end suppliers will not survive scrutiny. A lot structure chosen for the appearance of openness, but which cannot be assured or administered, will not work in operation. The chosen option has to be supported by evidence and simple to operate.
Position: one end-to-end lot · held in principle, rationale recorded · fixed with the sourcing strategy
